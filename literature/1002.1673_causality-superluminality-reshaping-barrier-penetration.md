# On causality, apparent 'superluminality' and reshaping in barrier penetration

**Authors:** D. Sokolovski

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1002.1673 - arXiv abstract page only; full text not retrieved

## Abstract

We consider tunnelling of a non-relativistic particle across a potential barrier. It is shown that the barrier acts as an effective beam splitter which builds up the transmitted pulse from the copies of the initial envelope shifted in the coordinate space backwards relative to the free propagation. Although along each pathway causality is explicitly obeyed, in special cases reshaping can result an overall reduction of the initial envelope, accompanied by an arbitrary coordinate shift. In the case of a high barrier the delay amplitude distribution (DAD) mimics a Dirac $δ$-function, the transmission amplitude is superoscillatory for finite momenta and tunnelling leads to an accurate advancement of the (reduced) initial envelope by the barrier width. In the case of a wide barrier, initial envelope is accurately translated into the complex coordinate plane. The complex shift, given by the first moment of the DAD, accounts for both the displacement of the maximum of the transmitted probability density and the increase in its velocity. It is argued that analysing apparent 'superluminality' in terms of spacial displacements helps avoid contradiction associated with time parameters such as the phase time.

## Why this matters here

Sokolovski's reshaping account, and the most concrete answer to whether the advance is genuine. He rewrites transmission as the action of an effective beam splitter: the transmitted pulse is a superposition of copies of the free envelope shifted backwards in coordinate space, weighted by a delay amplitude distribution obtained as the Fourier transform of the transmission amplitude. Each individual pathway is manifestly causal, no copy is ahead of free propagation, yet interference among them can reduce the envelope and displace the maximum by an arbitrary amount. In the high barrier limit the delay amplitude distribution mimics a Dirac delta, the transmission amplitude becomes superoscillatory over finite momentum ranges, and the net effect is an accurate advance of a strongly reduced envelope by exactly the barrier width, which is the Hartman result derived without any transit time. For a wide barrier the envelope is translated into the complex coordinate plane, and the first moment of the delay amplitude distribution, a complex shift, accounts jointly for the displaced maximum and for the increased apparent velocity. Sokolovski argues explicitly that analysing the effect as a spatial displacement avoids the contradictions that follow from treating the phase time as a duration. Directly against our reading: it says the phase we extract encodes a reshaping weight, not a clock, and the article must either accept that or show why the signature-changed region is different.
