# Superluminal tunneling times without superluminal signaling: Fading of the MacColl-Hartman effect at early times

**Authors:** Randall S. Dumont, Tom Rivlin

**Journal:** Phys. Rev. A 107 (2023) 052212

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15375 - arXiv abstract page only; full text not retrieved

## Abstract

A curious feature of quantum tunneling known as the MacColl-Hartman effect results in the numerical observation that particles can traverse a barrier with effective superluminal speed. However, because tunneling is never certain, any attempt to use this effect to send a signal faster than light would require sending many particles. In this work, we consider sending, in parallel, without interactions between particles, sufficiently many particles to ensure at the least one of them tunnels. In this case, in spite of the time advance of the mean time for a single tunneling particle, the mean time to send one bit of information is larger for tunneling particles than for the same number of free photons. This removes any possibility of superluminal signaling. We show that the mean time to send one bit using N particles is determined by the early-time tail of the distribution of tunneling times for one particle and that, when this early-time tail is highly accurately modeled using steepest descent, the MacColl-Hartman effect is seen to fade away.

## Why this matters here

This paper draws precisely the line our project needs between a superluminal timing observable and superluminal signalling, and it does so without denying the effect. Dumont and Rivlin accept the MacColl-Hartman advance for the mean arrival time of a single tunnelling particle, then ask how long it takes to actually send one bit, given that tunnelling is probabilistic and one must send N particles in parallel to be reasonably sure that at least one gets through. The time to first arrival among N particles is controlled by the early time tail of the single particle arrival time distribution, not by its mean, and they evaluate that tail by steepest descent. The result is that the mean time to transmit one bit with N tunnelling particles exceeds the time for the same number of free photons, so the MacColl-Hartman effect fades away exactly in the regime where signalling would be at stake. This is the cleanest available answer to whether an advanced peak can carry information: the peak is advanced, the bit is not. For our article this means the saturating two-crossing delay can be reported as a real feature of the amplitude while the signalling claim is explicitly withheld, and it supplies the mechanism, the early time tail, that makes the two statements compatible.
