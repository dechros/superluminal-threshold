# Bounds and enhancements for the Hartman effect

**Authors:** J. G. Muga, I. L. Egusquiza, J. A. Damborenea, F. Delgado

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0206181 - arXiv abstract page only; full text not retrieved

## Abstract

The time of passage of the transmitted wave packet in a tunneling collision of a quantum particle with a square potential barrier becomes independent of the barrier width in a range of barrier thickness. This is the Hartman effect, which has been frequently associated with ``superluminality''. A fundamental limitation on the effect is set by non-relativistic ``causality conditions''. We demonstrate first that the causality conditions impose more restrictive bounds on the negative time delays (time advancements) when no bound states are present. These restrictive bounds are in agreement with a naive, and generally false, causality argument based on the positivity of the ``extrapolated phase time'', one of the quantities proposed to characterize the duration of the barrier's traversal. Nevertheless, square wells may in fact lead to much larger advancements than square barriers. We point out that close to thresholds of new bound states the time advancement increases considerably, while, at the same time, the transmission probability is large, which facilitates the possible observation of the enhanced time advancement.

## Why this matters here

Muga and coworkers put quantitative bounds on how large the Hartman advance is allowed to be, which is the discipline our saturating delay needs. Working with a square barrier, they show that non-relativistic causality conditions on the scattering amplitude restrict negative time delays, that is time advancements, and that the restriction is markedly tighter when the potential supports no bound states. In that case the bound coincides with what a naive positivity argument for the extrapolated phase time would give, an argument they point out is in general false but happens to be correct here. The interesting asymmetry is that square wells, unlike square barriers, can produce much larger advancements, and that near the threshold at which a new bound state appears the advance grows considerably while the transmission probability stays large, which is the configuration in which an enhanced advance could actually be seen. This bears on us in two ways. Our signature-changed region is not a plain barrier, and if the effective problem it maps to admits bound or quasi-bound states then the bounds that constrain barrier advances are the looser ones, so the size of our delay is not automatically anomalous. It also tells us which quantity to compute alongside the delay, namely the bound state spectrum of the effective potential, before claiming that the advance is large.
